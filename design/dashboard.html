ICMC 2020 · Problem 1Analysed 30 Oct · 2 pages uploaded

Alice and Bob play a game on a sphere which is initially marked with a finite number of points. Alice and Bob then take turns making moves, with Alice going first:

  • On Alice's move, she counts the number of marked points on the sphere, n. She then marks another n+1 points on the sphere.
  • On Bob's move, he chooses one hemisphere and removes all marked points on that hemisphere, including any marked points on the boundary of the hemisphere.

Can Bob always guarantee that after a finite number of moves, the sphere contains no marked points?

(A hemisphere is the region on a sphere that lies completely on one side of any plane passing through the centre of the sphere.)

1 · Reading
Pólya (1945). Understand the problem before planning anything.
"including any marked points on the boundary of the hemisphere" rule · 95
The hemisphere is closed. Bob can put the plane through marked points and they count as removed. This is the lever.
Spotted because a clause that only matters in edge cases is usually there because the edge case is the point.
"marks another n+1 points" rule · 85
n becomes 2n+1, slightly more than doubling. Bob must remove more than half each round or the count never falls.
"any plane passing through the centre" definition · 75
Any two points on the sphere lie on a common plane through the centre, so Bob can always put two marked points on the boundary.
Definitions made explicit: hemisphere = closed half of the sphere cut by a plane through the centre; round = one Alice move then one Bob move; ck = count after Bob's k-th move.
2 · Goal
Pólya (1945). State what is asked and what the final line would say.

Asked. Whether Bob has a strategy that empties the sphere against every possible play by Alice.

Answer form. Yes or no, with proof. If yes, a strategy for Bob plus a proof it terminates. If no, an Alice strategy that keeps a point forever.

The final line would say. After every round the number of marked points strictly decreases, so after finitely many rounds it is zero.

3 · Informal cues
Pólya (1945). Small words carry the quantifiers: always, finite, first.
  • Alice going first: each round is add-then-remove, so the quantity to control is the count left after Bob.
  • always guarantee: Bob cannot rely on Alice placing points badly; the strategy must beat the worst case.
  • finite number of points: the count is a natural number from the first move, so descent is available.
4 · Abstraction
Chi, Feltovich & Glaser (1981). Experts sort by deep structure; novices by surface features.
sphere is really a closed surface with central symmetry and no boundary → every great circle passes through antipodal pairs; there is no edge for points to hide at.
hemisphere is really the sphere cut by a closed half-space whose plane contains the centre → its boundary is a great circle, and because it is closed, points on that circle are removed.
choosing a hemisphere is really choosing a plane through the centre, then a side → the two closed hemispheres cover every point, so one side contains at least half.

"At least half" only stops growth. "More than half" forces decrease. The boundary clause is what upgrades one to the other.

5 · Simplification
Pólya (1945). Solve a simpler related problem first.

Same game on a circle. Alice adds n+1 points; Bob removes a closed semicircle. Start with one point, Alice adds 2. Place the three points 120° apart and check whether any closed semicircle contains all three. Then ask how many marked points a diameter can pass through.

In two dimensions a diameter passes through at most one marked point and its antipode. On a sphere, how many marked points can a plane through the centre pass through, and what does the boundary clause let Bob do with that?

Monovariant: the count strictly decreases every round. Deciding cues: after a finite number of moves, marks another n+1 points, boundary of the hemisphere. A plane through two marked points removes at least n+2 of the 2n+1, leaving at most n−1.
dropped
Induction on n. The same argument in different clothing; the monovariant is cleaner to state.
dropped
An Alice strategy that keeps points forever. Any configuration is beaten by a plane through two of its points.
dropped
Potential function on positions. Far heavier than needed; the count alone suffices.
